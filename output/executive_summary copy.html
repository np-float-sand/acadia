The Thesis

Most utility quant factors — yield, regulatory lag, rate sensitivity — are well-known and well-arbitraged. This strategy exploits a different edge: utilities are systematically mispriced around grid stress events, and their sensitivity varies predictably by service territory, generation mix, and transmission exposure.

The core insight is that a utility's stress beta — how much its stock moves on high-grid-stress days relative to the sector — is a function of physical grid topology, not analyst estimates. It is therefore slow to be arbitraged away.

Long resilient operators (low stress beta). Short exposed operators (high stress beta). Rebalance monthly as betas evolve.

On raw vs. risk-adjusted returns: XLU buy-and-hold returned +113% over the same period, versus the strategy's +90%. But XLU's max drawdown was −36.1% (vs −13.7% here), and its return came with 20.6% annual volatility versus 13.2%. The strategy delivers a comparable return per unit of risk — with dramatically less downside exposure — making it suitable as a complement to, not replacement for, long-only utility holdings.

Finding 1 — Short Book Drives Alpha

The strategy's return comes primarily from short-book stock selection, not from long-only utility picking.

Every configuration using XLU as the short hedge produced negative Sharpe (all 81 XLU-hedge runs ranked below the worst individual-short configuration across 243 combinations tested). The long book's top-scored names do not outperform the sector ETF by enough to make a sector-relative trade work.

Implication: the edge lies in identifying specific losers during stress events — names with concentrated transmission or generation exposure — not in picking winners.

Finding 2 — Duration Beats Threshold

brief congestion spikes are common and unactionable; sustained congestion (>=10 consecutive days with congestion component at least 40% of electricity marginal price) is what reprices utility credit and equity risk.

Finding 3 — Concentrate the Long Book

For now with limited data, the long book is only 3 names. The strategy will need more breadth to be viable.

The result: max drawdown −13.7% vs −36.1% for XLU — a 2.6× drawdown reduction while the strategy still delivered 90% cumulative return. This asymmetry reflects the short book hedging correlated sector downturns, while the long book concentrates in the most resilient names.

Known Weaknesses

  • Data depth ~7 years covers three major stress regimes but not a full interest-rate cycle. Caution on rate-driven utility selloffs (2022–2023) where the short book partially hedged but the long book suffered.
  • Universe size 18 tickers. Liquidity and capacity are real constraints at institutional scale without expanding coverage.
  • Beta lag OLS betas are estimated on rolling windows. The factor may react slowly to structural changes (e.g. asset sales, service territory changes).
  • Data coverage SPP and CAISO LMP history is shorter than ERCOT/PJM, making stress betas for those utilities noisier.
  • Transaction costs Backtest assumes zero slippage. With 5 shorts and monthly rebalancing, real-world costs reduce the Sharpe, estimated 0.05–0.10 impact.

Future Work

  • Near-term Interest Coverage Ratio (ICR) as a 15% factor component — identifies financially fragile utilities entering stress events. Infrastructure built; awaiting IC validation on the short book.
  • Near-term RT/DA LMP spread as a 5th GSI sub-signal — real-time vs. day-ahead price spread cleanly separates generators (benefit from RT spikes) from T&D utilities (hurt by congestion charges). ~90% of infrastructure built.
  • Medium-term Expand universe to 30–40 names using EDGAR as backup data source, removing yfinance coverage limits and extending historical depth.
  • Medium-term Inter-zonal congestion spread — LMP component decomposition (energy / congestion / loss) to sharpen the congestion sub-signal beyond the current fraction proxy.
