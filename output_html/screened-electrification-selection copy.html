Screened Electrification Equipment — the selection book#

The same rules-based grid-supplier portfolio, now holding only the half of it that the evidence favours. Every name is still selected by rule from SEC filings; the new step ranks them by two facts in their own accounts and keeps the better half.

Window: January 2019 – August 2026 · The grid-tight period (January 2023 on) is reported separately throughout · Trading costs in Costs · Management fee not assumed

The idea in one paragraph#

Electricity demand is growing faster than the grid can carry it, and someone is being paid to build the difference. Our published book owns the suppliers being paid — equipment makers and electrical contractors that spend about 2 cents of each revenue dollar on physical assets, against a utility's 44 — and it deliberately did not try to pick winners among them: every qualifying name got equal money. This proposal drops that last restraint. Since the grid became genuinely tight in 2023, two facts in a supplier's own filings have separated the winners from the rest, and we now use them to hold only the stronger half.

What changes, and what does not#

Rules 1–5 of the published book are unchanged: suppliers not owners of the grid (low capital intensity), a company that says so in its own 10-K, profitable on the numbers known at the time, not dependent on subsidies, and liquid. Rules 7–9 — the risk overlay — are unchanged too.

Rule 6 is replaced. The published book held every qualifying name equally because we had no evidence we could rank them. We now do.

6. We hold the half of the list that scores best on two facts from the accounts.

  • How little the company spends on capital, relative to its sales. Within an already capital-light list, the lightest keep doing better: they convert the buildout into cash now rather than into assets that earn later.
  • Whether its revenue growth is speeding up. This year's growth rate minus last year's, on trailing-twelve-month revenue as filed. A supplier whose orders are accelerating is the one the buildout is reaching.

Each fact is ranked across the whole ~146-name eligible universe each month, the two ranks are averaged, and at each rebalance we hold the top half of the qualifying names, equally weighted. Both inputs are read from filings as published on the day — no restated numbers, no hindsight. The book holds 17 names today (14 on average since 2023), against 35 for the published book.

Why these two and not others. We tested fourteen candidate stock-ranking signals over the same universe — earnings surprises, revenue surprises, momentum, reversal, and the stocks' sensitivity to wholesale power prices, grid congestion and scarcity pricing. These were the only two with a consistent relationship to future returns, and the power-market signals — which sound the most relevant — ranked stocks no better than chance. We also tried adding links to related firms (10-K text similarity, shared headquarters city): none improved the result.

How we control risk#

Unchanged from the published book: 7. position size scaled to 20% target volatility; 8. size cut again when the book is extended far above its trend or the market; 9. 15% held in gold and Treasuries. The concentrated book needs these more, not less:

Drawdowns and market sensitivity#

The book carries 0.60 beta to the S&P 500 over the full period (0.74 since 2023) against 1.07 for GRID, and captures 78% of the market's up days against 69% of its down days.

Is this alpha?#

In fairness: yes against the grid-equipment index, in the regime it is built for — and most of that alpha was already in the published book. The selection step adds a smaller, less certain piece on top.

The test is a regression of the book's daily return on GRID's: whatever return is left over after accounting for the book's exposure to the theme is alpha. A t-statistic above 2 means the result is unlikely to be luck.

Three honest readings:

  1. Against GRID the alpha is real and statistically strong since 2022–23 (t above 3), and it is positive — though small — even in 2019–22. By the test we set for ourselves before starting this work (positive in both halves, t at least 1.5 over the whole period), it qualifies.
  2. Most of it is not new. The published book already beat GRID by 14.7% a year since 2023. That alpha comes from how the book is built — capital-light suppliers, the volatility target, the hedge sleeve — not from ranking names.
  3. The selection adds about 4 points a year since 2023, at t ≈ 1.6 — suggestive, not proven, and zero before 2022. It also carries a hindsight caveat: the two-factor score was chosen in September 2026 after testing fourteen candidates. We froze it on 24 September 2026 and record its picks every month from August 2026; that live record is the real test.

The hedged version: owning the alpha without the theme#

For an investor who already owns the theme, or wants the stock selection without the sector's swings, the book can be run short an index ETF against it. Since 2023, hedging out the theme still leaves 17% a year at under 10% volatility, with the worst drawdown between −6% and −9% depending on the hedge. Over the full period the hedged book earns 10–13% a year; nearly all of it arrives after 2022.

Can you short GRID? Yes. GRID is an $11.8bn fund trading about $100m a day, with listed options; large ETFs are normally cheap to borrow, but we have not verified GRID's borrow rate, which is why we charge 0.5%. PAVE ($13.5bn, $82m a day) and XLI ($32bn, $1.3bn a day) are easier still, and on these numbers PAVE is the better hedge. An institutional investor would more often use a total-return swap on the index than a physical short.

Hedged Sharpes use the same 4% risk-free rate and are not credited with interest on the short proceeds, which understates them slightly.

Risks#

The selection was chosen with hindsight. The two-factor score was picked in September 2026 after fourteen candidates were tested on this same history, and "hold the top half" was one of several ways of using it. The backtest therefore flatters it. The score has been frozen since 24 September 2026 and its picks are logged every month; until that live record exists, treat the selection's extra 4 points a year as an estimate, not a track record.

It only works once the grid is tight. In 2019–22 the selection added nothing (−0.4% a year against the published book) and cost 2020 dearly. If the buildout slows and capital intensity and revenue acceleration stop distinguishing suppliers, the book reverts to a more concentrated — and therefore riskier — version of the published one.

It is concentrated, and was very concentrated early. 17 names today and 14 on average since 2023, but only 3–4 in 2019 and as few as one at a rebalance. The early figures, good and bad, rest on a handful of stocks. The full-period drawdown (−24.1% against −20.0%) is the cost of that concentration.

The hedge is an estimate. Hedge size comes from a trailing beta that can lag a regime change, and the borrow cost is assumed, not observed.

We still have no exit signal. Nothing here tells us when the buildout ends; the overlay limits what a downturn costs, it does not predict one.
