Survivorship bias check — one-score long/short#

Tested 2026-10-02 · backtest window January 2019 – August 2026

The question#

The backtest draws its stocks from SEC filers in power and electrical-equipment industry codes, priced from a data source that only carries companies still listed today. Companies that went bankrupt, were taken over or delisted during the backtest are missing. If the strategy would have bought losers or shorted takeover targets among them, the reported results are too good.

To test this we found the missing companies, priced them, put them back in the pool, and re-ran the strategy unchanged.

Headline#

Putting the missing companies back barely changes the result. Sharpe moves by 0.07 or less in every period, with or without the step-aside rule.

Which companies were missing#

Of 2,571 SEC filers in the strategy's industry codes, 2,196 have no stock ticker today. Most disappeared before 2016. 229 dropped out during the backtest years; we could match and price 57 of them that were still trading after January 2019. Those 57 were added to the pool.

What the strategy did with them#

The added names pass through the same rules as every other stock — trading-volume floor, screen, fourteen-signal score — so most never qualified.

  • 21 of 57 passed the $10 million-a-day trading-volume floor at some point.
  • 8 were actually traded: 2 on the long side, 7 on the short side (Barnes on both, at different times).
  • Average names held moved from 9.9 to 10.3 (long) and 16.4 to 17.6 (short).

Why the effect is small#

Almost every missing company that qualified was a utility, pipeline or coal producer. They spend heavily on plant, so they fail the long book's capital-light screen and land in the short pool. Most of them later left through a takeover at a premium, which costs a short seller money. So survivorship here mainly removes takeover targets from the short book, not bankruptcies from the long book. The long book picked only Barnes, which made money.

Caveats#

  • Prices for delisted companies come from Tiingo. Each series is cut at the company's exit to avoid ticker re-use (for example, "B" now belongs to Barrick Gold). Barnes's trading-volume figure looks inflated by this, but Barnes qualifies either way.
  • Exits during 2026 were not checked. A company that filed its 2025 annual report and was then taken over this year falls outside both lists.
  • Re-registered companies (a new holding company or a blank-check merger) can look alive under a new SEC number while their old history is lost.
  • A full fix needs a survivorship-free database (CRSP or equivalent) that includes delisting returns.

How it was done#

  1. Pulled every annual-report filer in the strategy's 28 industry codes from SEC EDGAR, and kept those with no ticker today that filed an annual report in 2016 or later.
  2. Recovered each one's old ticker from its last annual report, checked against Tiingo's listing dates, and downloaded daily prices and trading volume.
  3. Added them, with their SEC financial data and annual-report wording, to a separate copy of the data. The production data was not changed.
  4. Re-ran the whole strategy unchanged on that copy, with and without the step-aside rule.
  5. Control: the same re-run without the added companies reproduces the reported figures exactly (Sharpe 1.341 / 0.436 / 2.053), so every difference above comes from the added companies.
