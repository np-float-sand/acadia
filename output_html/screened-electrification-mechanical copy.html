Screened Electrification Equipment — the mechanical book#

A long-only portfolio of the companies paid to build out the electricity grid. Every name is selected by rule from SEC filings. There is no hand-written list.

Window: January 2019 – August 2026 · Trading costs estimated in Costs; management fee not assumed

The idea#

Electricity demand is growing faster than the grid can carry it. Data centres, electrification of transport and heat, and the retirement of older generation are all arriving at once, and the wires, transformers and substations in between were built for a smaller world.

Someone has to pay for that. We would rather own the companies being paid than the companies paying.

A regulated utility spends about 44 cents of every revenue dollar on physical assets, then waits a decade — and a regulator's approval — to earn a return on it. The equipment makers and electrical contractors selling it transformers, switchgear and installation labour spend about 2 cents. They get paid now, in cash, at the top of the cycle. Same boom, opposite sides of the balance sheet.

That ~20× gap in capital intensity is the heart of the strategy, and it is a fact in the accounts rather than a judgment call — which is what lets the whole portfolio be built by rule.

Why we own what we own#

1. We buy suppliers to the grid, not owners of the grid. We keep only companies whose capital spending is low relative to sales. This is the "receives the buildout money" test, and it separates a contractor from a utility without anyone having to decide which is which.

2. A company has to tell us, in its own filings, that this is its business. We include a firm only if its annual report discusses data centres, substations, switchgear, transmission or grid modernisation. We use the company's own legally-binding disclosure rather than a sector label, because labels are broad and stale — "Industrials" holds hundreds of firms with no grid exposure at all.

3. It has to have been profitable, judged on what was knowable at the time. This theme attracts story stocks — firms promising grid revenue that never arrives. Requiring real reported earnings screens them out. We use the earnings as published on the day, never as later restated, so we never credit ourselves with knowledge we could not have had.

4. We avoid businesses that depend on subsidies. If a firm's filings lean on the investment tax credit or net metering, you are not only buying electricity demand — you are buying an election result. We want the structural driver.

5. It has to be liquid enough to actually trade. Every name must have traded at least $10m of stock on a typical day over the past year. A portfolio you cannot enter or exit at scale is not a portfolio, it is a chart.

6. We do not back our own stock-picking within the list. Every name gets equal money, capped at 25%, rebalanced quarterly. We have confidence in the screen; we have no evidence we can rank winners inside it, so we do not pretend to.

Today that produces a 35-name portfolio. Every filter reads only documents published before the year it is deciding, so the portfolio you see for any past date is one you could have actually held on that date.

How we control risk#

The stock selection produces a volatile book. Three layers sit on top of it, and together they cut the worst loss by more than half.

7. We cut size when the book gets volatile. Position size is scaled to target 20% annual volatility. This is the single most valuable thing we do — and it works automatically, without anyone having to call a top.

8. We cut size again when the book gets expensive. When the portfolio has run far above its own trend or dramatically ahead of the market, we step exposure down. This is not a forecast that the theme is over; it is a refusal to hold maximum size after prices have already moved a long way in our favour.

9. We hold 15% in gold and Treasuries. It costs roughly 2% a year in return and buys the last 3 points of drawdown protection. It earns its place by being uncorrelated with everything else we own, precisely when that matters.

10. The portfolio we are proposing is long only. Shorting the capital-heavy utilities as a hedge lost money in every period tested and fell alongside the portfolio on bad days — a position correlating +0.60 with what you are hedging is not insurance. Downside protection comes from 7 and 9. We have since found one short that does work on the numbers, and it is described below — we are not including it, for reasons of borrow rather than performance.

Drawdowns#

The book sat well above the market's drawdown line in the two big shocks: −19.4% against −33.7% in the 2020 crash and −17.8% against −24.5% in 2022. Its own worst moment — −20.0%, bottoming in June 2022 — came from a slide that began in late 2021 and spanned both calendar years.

One exception, in fairness: in the 2025 tariff selloff the book fell −19.3% against the S&P's −18.8%. It has not protected in every episode, and that one it did not.

Risk and return against the alternatives#

The thematic ETFs (GRID, PAVE) reach similar returns by taking 24–26% volatility. The book reaches 22.4% on 16.8%. That difference is the risk overlay, not the stock picking.

Two costs matter: what we pay to trade, and what a manager charges. The first is now estimated; the second is a decision, so what follows is the budget available for it.

The reason is visible in the last column: a fast estimate is what lets the book cut exposure inside a crash rather than after it. Trading frequently is not an oversight here — it is the product.

How precise these figures are#

The turnover numbers are exact — they come from the simulated positions. The cost per unit traded is an assumption: a fixed spread with no market-impact term, which understates the cost of a large book. That is why the table shows a range rather than a single number, and why the conclusion is framed on the 20–30bp columns as well as the 10bp one. The $10m-a-day liquidity floor makes low single-digit basis points plausible for a book of this size; it does not prove it, and real fills would.

One implementation decision is still open, and it is worth more than any refinement to the screen. At 594% turnover a year, resizing the book is 85% of the bill. Done by trading the 35 underlying names it costs ~0.6%/yr; done in index futures or a liquid ETF — which is the normal way to run a volatility target — it costs closer to 0.1%, and the net Sharpe moves from 1.008 to about 1.04. Nothing else on the table has that leverage.

The short leg we tested#

Most people expect a strategy like this to have a short side, so it is worth saying exactly what we found. We set the bar before running anything: a short basket must raise both Sharpe and Calmar, or it does not go in.

Eight candidates were tested. Seven failed. One passed, clearly.

What works — and why the earlier attempts didn't#

The winning basket is the most capital-intensive companies in the industry that have also been unprofitable for twelve consecutive months. These are firms funding heavy capital spending out of losses — a structurally poor place to be — and the market has treated them accordingly: the basket fell 30% a year on its own.

To be precise about what "most capital-intensive" is measured against: the ranking runs across the whole eligible universe of roughly 146 names a month, not across the 35 we own. It has to — the portfolio is by construction the capital-light end, so there is no heavy quartile inside it. The two legs are mutually exclusive by definition, and the short reaches into ground the long book excludes on purpose: five of the nineteen names ever shorted are utilities or pipelines. This is therefore not a market-neutral pair trade. It is a long book of capital-light suppliers plus a separate short of capital-heavy loss-makers — two independent bets that happen to combine well.

That is the difference. Every earlier attempt shorted something chosen because it moved opposite the portfolio. This one shorts something that simply goes down — and a short only funds itself if the thing you are shorting has negative expected return.

It costs almost nothing to trade: about 0.03% a year on top of the existing bill.

Making it less concentrated#

Three names is too few to trade, so we tried to widen it. Loosening the universe turns out to be pointless — the eligible pool is already as wide as the data allows (134 names against 135 if we drop the industry gate entirely; liquidity is doing all the filtering). What works is loosening the capital-intensity cut:

The heaviest-40% version is the best of these: half again as many names for 0.067 of Sharpe, and it still tolerates a 20% borrow cost. Loosening the loss test is the worse dial — twelve months of losses is what produces the negative drift, and dropping it to a single quarter cuts the basket's standalone decline from −21% a year to −10%.

Why we are not including it#

It is one sector's bust, not a rule — and the evidence is emphatic. The obvious objection is that the basket's decline comes almost entirely from five hydrogen and fuel-cell names. On its own that proves nothing: a screen hunting capital-heavy loss-makers in the electricity sector should find hydrogen companies, because that is exactly what they were. Removing them removes the signal, not a bias.

The fair test is whether the same screen picks losers in industries with nothing to do with the grid. We ran it across a thousand-company, all-sector universe. It does the opposite:

universe qualifying names a month what the basket did
Electricity only — where the rule was found 4.9 −21% a year
Every other sector, identical rule 20.8 +52% a year
Other sectors, capital-heavy but profitable (control) 148 +16% a year

Outside the grid, companies funding heavy capital spending out of sustained losses are biotech, AI infrastructure and early-stage hardware — and between 2019 and 2026 they beat their profitable peers by 36 points a year. Shorting them would have been ruinous. So the screen does not identify structurally bad businesses; it identifies speculative growth, which normally rises. It worked in electricity only because that was the one sector whose speculative bubble deflated while the rest of the market's kept inflating.

It cannot be run at size. The thinnest name in the basket trades about $10m a day. Holding a short position without becoming a meaningful share of daily volume caps the whole fund at roughly $73m — and that is at a fairly aggressive 10% participation rate. This is a small-fund trade at best.

Borrowing these shares is expensive. They are among the most heavily shorted stocks in the market — a current snapshot shows a median 24% of free float already sold short, SERV at 37% and EOSE at 31%. The position survives a 20%-a-year borrow cost but fails at 40%, and we have no historical borrow series to say which side of that line the real world sat on.

The honest summary: this is the first short leg in this research ever to pass a pre-registered bar, and it is the clearest example of why passing one is not enough. Every mechanical check agreed with it — the position size didn't matter, both halves of the period worked, it survived a simulated 100% squeeze. None of those checks was capable of detecting the problem. Only asking "does this work anywhere else?" did, and the answer was that the same rule makes 52% a year on the other side of the trade. The book stays long only.

Risks#

This is a concentrated bet on one theme, not a diversified equity portfolio. Correlation to the S&P is 0.72 and to the grid ETFs higher still. It will fall in a broad selloff; the overlay is designed to make it fall less, not to make it uncorrelated.

We have no exit signal. Rules 7–9 limit what a downturn costs, but nothing here tells us when the buildout ends. We have tested more than a dozen candidate early-warning signals built on grid data — congestion pricing, capacity auctions, load forecasts, utility capital-spending guidance — and none showed a usable relationship with future returns. We regard the structural tilt as sound and our ability to time it as unproven.

The early years are thin. The portfolio held 7–8 names through 2019 and did not reach 20 until 2022. The strong early ratios rest on a small book, and should be discounted accordingly.

2022 is the honest blemish. In the one down year, the hand-picked version lost less (−0.39 Sharpe versus −0.63). A rule-based book does not get to step aside from a bad year.
